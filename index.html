Prologue

Before the water

It started with twelve seconds of someone else’s reel — a drowned rotunda by simonzhang.art: ivy, green water, a woman in marble standing very still in the middle of it all. I watched it more times than I’d like to admit. Not for the light. For her.

Who builds a round room, floods it, and leaves exactly one person inside?

This sketchbook is my answer: a myth, a rule, a lot of stone and a few billion triangles’ worth of trying. Everything in it was built from nothing — the arches, the ivy, her face — with code first, then Blender, then Unreal, and now this page. Some of it took a day. Her face took five tries.

Chapter I

The Spring

Before the walls there was a spring that never froze. The valley called it the Eye. They ringed it with twelve bays — one for every month the Eye had kept them alive — and roofed it with a dome pierced sixteen times, so the sun could find the water at any hour.

A ring has no corners to hide in. That was the point.

II

Chapter II

Twelve Bays

Romanesque, but not pure. Twin columns with cushion capitals, arches stepping back in three orders like ripples, a gallery above, a cornice that drips. Every course of ashlar is actually cut — eight-millimetre joints in the geometry, not painted on — because stone that’s laid catches the light differently from stone that’s printed.

The making of · stone

From a cube to a cloister

Every stone here is generated by code. A small Python mesh toolkit came first — it could make a cube and a column, nothing else. From there: a straight arcade, bent around the circle; the joints carved into it; then weathering, as masks for moss, grime and the waterline. The same masks drive Blender, Unreal and the live world on this page.

III

Chapter III

The Night of Bells

In the year the chronicles call the Night of Bells, the ground moved. Every bell in the valley rang with no hand on the rope. The dome held — but its sixteen eyes cracked into wounds, the drain under the Eye collapsed, and the spring, with nowhere left to go, began to fill the ring.

By morning the floor was gone. By winter the thirteen steps down to it were gone too.

Chapter IV

La Custode

She was carved for the plinth over the Eye: a woman pressing a folded sheet to her chest — the gesture of someone keeping something dry. After the flood they found her turned. Not toppled. Turned — to face the door.

Every keeper since has written the same line in the log: she faces whoever comes in.

The making of · La Custode

Five faces

She is a signed-distance field: capsules, folds and a skull blended together with smooth minimums, then meshed into 1.2 million triangles. At full resolution she ran out of memory — fourteen full-size arrays held at once for one blend — so the blend learned to stream, slab by slab.

The harder problem wasn’t memory. It was her expression. A statue that waits has to look like she’s waiting, and every face I made looked like she was doing something else.

Chapter V

Green Water

The water isn’t dirty. It’s alive — algae fed by seventeen shafts of sun, and copper weeping from the old roof cramps. Green is the colour of a room that’s still breathing.

I keyed every frame around it: stone neutral, sun warm, and the water the only loud thing in the room.

Chapter VI

What Grows

Ivy climbs toward the eyes. Moss takes the ledges where the drip lands. Ferns find the joints. Nothing grows on her — the keepers say she shrugs it off; I say it’s the marble. (All right, a little moss. She’s eight hundred years old.)

The making of · ivy

Sticks, then leaves

Alpha cards are the usual trick for ivy: flat quads with a picture of leaves on them. Seen edge-on they collapse into lines — and my walls looked like they were covered in twigs. Two days of “why does it look like a broom”.

The fix was brute force. Every card became real leaves: eight shapes, bent, veined and scattered along the vines — 354,150 of them, each one a little different in size, angle and colour.

moss: 185k cushions, only where water drips — ledges, capitals, the tops of things.

VII

Chapter VII

No Entry — why?

Here is the rule. Every time someone steps through the door, the water rises — one finger’s width. Nobody knows why.

The keepers measured it for two hundred years on the back of that board: one scratch per visitor. That’s what the sign is for. It isn’t there to keep you out. It’s there to keep her from drowning.

The day the water reaches her hands, she stops holding it.

And when this page loaded, she turned to look at you. You’ve already come in.

The making of · what broke

Everything that went wrong
(a partial list)

01

Inside-out walls

Bending the straight arcade around the circle mirrored it, and every face quietly turned inside out. Ivy grew inside the stone. Scatter landed on the undersides of ledges.

fix: flip on bend + a winding check on every export.

02

Ivy made of sticks

Alpha cards, edge-on. See page 5.

fix: 354,150 real leaves.

03

Her smile

Version two smiled at nothing, like a shop mannequin. A statue that waits can’t smile.

fix: neutral mouth, open eyes, v5.

04

Out of memory

The full-resolution statue needed fourteen full-size arrays at once and the machine simply killed it.

fix: a streaming smooth-max, evaluated in slabs.

05

First light in Unreal

Yellow fog, waxy marble, neon-green water. The subsurface was far too strong and the fog far too warm.

first light is always wrong. that’s what it’s for.

06

1.55 billion

The final scene: 7.9 million unique triangles and 597,140 instances, about 1.55 billion triangles once every leaf is counted.
